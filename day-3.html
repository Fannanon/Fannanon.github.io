Day 3 – El Dorado Hills to Cooks Station

Monday, 11th April 2016

I left Alan and Susan's with a bit of a heavy heart. I had such a nice time with those guys, just hanging out and taking it easy. Before I hit the road we took a quick selfie using Susan's selfie-stick which it seemed she was not too proud to own. Came in handy in this instance though! I left the house the same way I arrived, with a hug. This time I wasn't dripping with sweat.

One of the most dangerous parts of my ride was waiting just down the road. The ACA maps guide you down El Dorado Hills Bvd to the point where the US50 crosses over the road. There is a shoulder here and indeed, there is also one of the old 'share the road' signs advising motorists to keep an eye out for cyclists. The problem is that as the road goes under US50' the bike lane abruptly ends. It then ejects you into the path of traffic exiting the freeway via a looped off ramp. The traffic enters the boulevard at high speed and I doubt very much that they're expecting to see a crazy guy on a bike. I only needed to try this once during my week in town to declare 'never again'. To any westbound tourists, I'd recommend leaving the road before you get to the US50 and just take the footpath. It's a lot slower but a lot less terrifying too!

The ride out to the small town of Plymouth took me through some lush green valleys through which a number of creeks ran leading to the Cosumnes River down the road. My head was filled with thoughts of the immense climb to come and I found it hard to really appreciate the beauty around me. Crossing the river itself provided a fantastic sight however. It ran strong, presumably powered by the snowmelt up in Sierra Nevada, cascading over the rocky riverbed in a white foam and through the valley it had created over the millenia. I really wanted to grab a photo but there just wasn't a safe enough place to stop and get my phone out. The bridge over the river curved and had a very minimal shoulder so I thought it best to keep on moving and minimise the risk.

I stopped briefly in Plymouth and grabbed a quart of Powerade. I reckon this was the best decision I made all day. That sugary goodness lasted all the way to camp and did a great job keeping me away from the bonk. I downed a muesli bar and hit the road once again, heading in the direction of Fiddletown Road, a quiet little stretch that would take me some 3000 feet up toward Carson Pass. The first hill was an absolute shocker. The grade was steep and the road curved, creating a blind corner for traffic coming from behind. I gnashed my teeth and churned through every gear until finally I was spinning in the granny gear. Suddenly, half way up the hill the rotation of my pedals came to an abrupt halt! I managed to unclip, avoiding a dangerous fall into the ditch on the side of the road and upon inspecting the bike, noticed that the chain had fallen into the spokes of my rear wheel. Fortunately I was able to slacken the chain and yank it out and back onto the cassette, covering my fingers in grease in the process. I span the pedals and the chain fell back into gear with no fuss. Phew! I guess the lower limit on the rear dérailleur must have been a bit too low, something to investigate later. I figured I'd just avoid the largest cog in the meantime.

The ascent began in earnest and I tried to settle into the groove. The mind is definitely the weak link when faced with a huge task like ascending a huge mountain pass. For no reason, the Gerry and the Pacemakers song and Liverpool FC anthem 'You'll Never Walk Alone' came to mind. It bloody helped! I don't know how or why but it really did. I soon forgot about the job and just got on with it. As I climbed, the scenery around me changed from the rural pastures of the lowlands to a more densely forested alpine environment. I passed through Fiddletown itself, which seemed like little more than a ghost town, glory days of the old west long since past.

After an hour or two (I wasn't keeping track) I reached an intersection at about 4000 feet where a choice between an easy or harder option had to be made. I was feeling okay but was definitely beginning to flag a little. A turn to the right would lead me down hill to the historic town of Volcano, where a camp site, pretty town and a pub with nice food awaited. A turn to the left would take me another 1000 feet up the mountain to Cooks Station, a roadhouse on the side of US88. If I chose Volcano, it would mean losing a fair amount of hard fought elevation and would add an extra 9km or so to the next day's ride. If I chose Cooks Station, I'd save time on the way up the mountain but would need to ascend another 1000ft. Struck by indecisiveness I elected to leave it up to the flip of a quarter, heads for Cooks, tails for Volcano. The first flip came up for Cooks and my heart sank a little. Maybe I really wanted to chill in Volcano? I flipped again for best two outta three and George Washington came up again. Cooks Station it was - we do not question the coin!

The ascent from here became near constant with very little in the way of flat or downhill sections. I entered a trance state, just focussing on keeping my breath steady and doing anything but looking at my dismal speed on the computer. The road ran up the side of a large hill. To my right was a wall of crumbling mud with the roots of great trees breaking through. To my left was a precipitous drop down a steep embankment. I was glad to be on the right. I came upon US88 and stopped for a breather triggering an immense cramp in my quads. I was definitely close to my limits and there was still a solid 4.5 miles of climbing before I'd reach Cooks Station.

The next hour was spent in a blur of sweat, melting sunscreen and longing for the end. I trudged up the ascent at a snails pace as traffic flew past me on the left. I may have wished for the comfort of a car in that moment and wondered if it would really be so bad to jump in the back of one of the many huge utes for the last stretch. Perish the thought! Soon enough I reached Cooks Station and a sign advising me that I'd hit 5000 feet. The clouds grazed the treetops and a light rain fell around me. The station was deserted and the gas pumps covered. The sign in the door read closed, nobody would be there until 7am the next day. Maybe I should have followed my heart to Volcano!

I looked around for a water tap to restock my dwindling supply but found no source of potable water. I figured my best bet was to check in with the neighbour. I walked over to the house and noted the sign advising me of a mad dog and equally mad house. I opened the screen door and the dog inside went absolutely mental. I didn't even knock. As I walked away, the home owner appeared in his truck! He immediately offered me water, all I had to do was assist him with backing his truck into the driveway. Turned out he's the son of the Cooks Station owner and is a fellow 1984 kid. Hooray! I topped up my water bladder, content in the knowledge that I'd at least be able to 'enjoy' some cinnamon oats for dinner and not die of dehydration.

So here I am, perched not quite on top of the world, but kinda close to it. It's as high as I've ever been, in the elevation sense of that word. I've got a cup of tea, a snickers bar and phone reception. Life is good. Judging by my map, the worst of the climbing comes tomorrow, but I'm hoping it goes quickly! Hoping for a nice breakfast before I head off too!
